It is a peculiar feature of modern life that we speak of "finding yourself" as though the self were buried somewhere, intact, waiting to be excavated. A more honest metaphor might be construction rather than excavation. No one finds a self. Every self is built, slowly and mostly without consent, out of the cultural materials available at the time and place of one's arrival.

This is not a diminishment. A house is not less real for having been built rather than found fully formed in the ground. But it does mean that the language, gestures, tastes, and assumptions we experience as most intimately *ours* were, almost without exception, handed to us by people who came before — parents, teachers, the accumulated weight of a language we did not choose to be born into.

## Inheritance Without Consent

Culture is unusual among inheritances in that it arrives before we are capable of evaluating it. By the time a person is old enough to ask whether they agree with the values they were raised inside, those values have already shaped the instrument doing the asking. This creates a peculiar bind: you cannot step fully outside your own formation in order to judge it, because the thing doing the judging is itself a product of that formation.

This does not mean cultural inheritance is inescapable or that critique is impossible — people revise, reject, and rebuild the cultures they were handed all the time. It means the revision always happens from somewhere, using tools that were themselves inherited. There is no neutral ground to stand on while deciding which parts of your inheritance to keep.

## The Myth of the Self-Made Life

The idea of a person who owes nothing to their surroundings — who built every value and habit from first principles, alone — is one of the more persistent fictions a culture can tell about itself, and not coincidentally, it is a fiction certain cultures find more flattering than others. It obscures the enormous, invisible labor of the people and institutions that made a given life possible: the language a person thinks in, the stories that taught them what counts as a good outcome, the quiet assumptions about time, effort, and worth absorbed before the age of ten.

> To recognize what you inherited is not to diminish what you have made of it.

## Making Something of What You Are Given

None of this argues for passivity. A person does not simply repeat their inheritance; they select from it, combine it with other inheritances they encounter later, and produce something that, while never wholly original, is genuinely theirs in the way a musician's interpretation of an old song is genuinely theirs, without needing to have invented the song.

The cultural life of a human being, then, is not a contradiction between authenticity and inheritance. It is what authenticity actually looks like, once you take seriously that no one starts from nothing.
