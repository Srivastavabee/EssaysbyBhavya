Around 3000 BCE, pastoralists from the steppe north of the Black and Caspian Seas began moving outward. They left no monuments and no inscriptions. They left a genetic signature instead, still readable in the ancestry of South Asia, where the Rigveda was composed, and of northern Europe, where *Beowulf* would one day be told.

Two of the oldest literary traditions on Earth, one born beside Indian rivers and one in the cold of the North Sea, share a thread of descent and have no memory of each other. History runs in civilizational lanes. Genetics does not know the lanes exist. The Rigveda seems to have suspected as much:

> एकं सद् विप्रा बहुधा वदन्ति  
> *ekaṃ sad viprā bahudhā vadanti*  
> Truth is one; the wise speak of it in many ways. — Rigveda 1.164.46

## One Habit of Mind

Both cultures built a rigorous relationship to inheritance before "science" existed as a word. Indian physicians, in the Charaka and Sushruta Samhitas, described conception through *beeja*, the seed, shaped by nourishment, suitability and disposition: no single factor makes a child, only their interaction. Old English culture had meter instead. *Beowulf* was carried for generations by rhythm alone, while Vedic *Shiksha* policed the pitch, length and breath of every verse, because a mispronounced verse was considered broken, like a corrupted line of code.

> A civilization does not need writing to remember precisely. It needs a structure worth trusting more than memory alone.

## How the Beginning Was Told

Read in the order they were made, the two traditions ask the same first question: where does life come from? The Rigveda answers first, perhaps around 1500 BCE, with an image that a biologist can hardly miss:

> हिरण्यगर्भः समवर्तताग्रे भूतस्य जातः पतिरेक आसीत् ।  
> स दाधार पृथिवीं द्यामुतेमां कस्मै देवाय हविषा विधेम ॥  
> *hiraṇyagarbhaḥ samavartatāgre bhūtasya jātaḥ patir eka āsīt |*  
> *sa dādhāra pṛthivīṃ dyām utemāṃ kasmai devāya haviṣā vidhema*  
> In the beginning arose the Golden Womb, the sole lord of all that exists. He held the earth and this sky. Which god shall we honour with our offering? — Rigveda 10.121.1

Hiranyagarbha is an embryo: existence imagined as something that grows outward from a single seed.

More than two thousand years later, in the seventh century, the cowherd-poet Cædmon was said to have been given the first song in English. *Cædmon's Hymn* is the oldest surviving Old English poem, and it imagines the same beginning through a different faculty. Life is not grown but spoken:

> *Nu sculon herigean heofonrices Weard...*  
> *He ærest sceop eorðan bearnum heofon to hrofe, halig Scyppend.*  
> Now we must praise the Guardian of the heavenly kingdom... He first made heaven as a roof for the children of earth, the holy Creator.

An embryo in one tradition, a word in the other. The Old English *Genesis A* later tells the same story with life given to humanity as breath (*gast*). Both are early attempts to say what we still cannot fully say.

Then comes *Beowulf*, set down in the manuscript around 1000 CE, and its idea of origin is a bloodline. Its monsters are not random. They descend from Cain, a line marked by inheritance:

> *eotenas ond ylfe ond orcneas, swylce gigantas, þa wið Gode wunnon...*  
> ettins and elves and evil spirits, and the giants too, who fought against God for a long time... — *Beowulf*, lines 112–114

Here heredity is already destiny, which is exactly the move the next section has to untangle.

## One Question, Different Answers

Once a society has a theory of inheritance, it must decide what to do with it. India's answer was divided. *Varna* tied a person's nature and duties to birth, which turned heredity into social architecture: a true intuition (traits are inherited) stretched into a false claim (traits determine worth). The same error returned in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as eugenics, speaking the language of science instead of scripture.

Yet the tradition argued with itself. Vedantic and later Bhakti thought held that the self is untouched by birth, and poets like Kabir attacked lineage-based hierarchy directly. The same rigor went into logic, where Nyaya set rules for valid inference; into matter, where Vaisheshika reasoned its way to atoms without instruments; into grammar, with Panini; and into the *Natyashastra*, an early theory of why drama moves an audience.

## What This Refuses to Let Us Believe

None of this means the ancients secretly held modern genetics or physics under other names. That reading flatters the past by erasing what is interesting: very different frameworks, with very different tools, arrived at the same discipline. Look closely, refuse the easy answer, build something that survives scrutiny. This is science in the deep sense, wearing vocabulary we no longer recognise.

What the blood remembers and what the verse remembers are the same kind of memory: an inheritance too precise to be accidental, carried by whatever structure a civilization had at hand, whether a genome, a meter or a grammar. The line we draw between biology and literature is younger than either. This journal exists to keep forgetting it.
