The oldest hymn in the Rigveda does not begin with a god. It begins with an absence:

> नासदासीन्नो सदासीत्तदानीं नासीद्रजो नो व्योमा परो यत् ।  
> *nāsad āsīn no sad āsīt tadānīm nāsīd rajo no vyomā paro yat*  
> Then there was neither non-being nor being; no air-space, nor sky beyond it. — Rigveda 10.129.1

Whoever wrote that was trying to imagine the time before anything. Science has a different vocabulary for the same attempt, and a surprising amount to say about it.

## Before the Earth

About 13.8 billion years ago, the universe began as something unimaginably hot and dense, and it has been expanding ever since. For a long time there were no stars, no planets, only hydrogen and helium drifting in the dark. Then gravity gathered the gas into the first stars. Inside them, simple atoms were fused into heavier ones: carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, iron. When those stars died, they scattered these elements across space.

Hold that thought, because it is the quiet centre of this story. The carbon in your cells and the oxygen you breathe were made in stars that died before the Sun was born.

## A Planet Out of Dust

About 4.6 billion years ago, a cloud of gas and dust, enriched by those dead stars, collapsed under its own weight. Most of it fell into the middle and became the Sun. What was left spun into a flat disc, and in that disc, grains stuck to grains, then pebbles to pebbles, then rocks to rocks, until there were planets.

The Earth was born in this way, and it was not gentle. It grew by being struck, again and again, and the heat of those impacts melted it. Early on, a Mars-sized body is thought to have crashed into it, throwing out debris that gathered into the Moon. For a while, the surface of our planet was a sea of molten rock.

## The World Without Anyone

Geologists call this first age the Hadean, after Hades, and the name is fair. Picture a planet with no continents as we know them, a sky the colour of rust or smoke, volcanoes everywhere, and the Moon hanging much closer and larger than it does now. There was no oxygen to breathe. The air was mostly carbon dioxide, nitrogen and water vapour.

Then it cooled, and it rained. For a very long time it rained, until the water gathered into oceans. Some tiny crystals of zircon found in Western Australia suggest that liquid water may have existed more than 4.3 billion years ago, far earlier than anyone expected. The early Earth may have been less hellish, and more watery, than its name suggests.

Still, there was no one there. No bird, no root, no moss. Only rock, water, wind and the slow chemistry of a planet finding its shape. For hundreds of millions of years, the Earth was a place that nothing would ever see.

## How Did It Begin to Wake?

This is the part that no one knows for certain, and an honest essay should say so. We have good ideas, not final answers.

Life needed a few things: water, a source of energy, and the right building blocks. In 1952, Stanley Miller sent sparks through a flask of simple gases and water, a stand-in for the early Earth, and found that amino acids, the units that make proteins, appeared on their own. Later work suggests that deep-sea hydrothermal vents, where hot mineral-rich water meets cold ocean, could have offered exactly the energy and chemistry that early life needed. Meteorites also carry organic molecules, so some of the ingredients may have come from space.

Next came a quieter leap, from chemistry to copying. Many scientists favour the idea of an "RNA world", in which simple molecules could both store information and speed up reactions. Once something could make imperfect copies of itself, evolution had begun, and the difference between a chemical and a creature started to blur.

The oldest convincing signs of life we have are layered rock structures made by microbes, about 3.5 billion years old, with disputed hints of much earlier life. It is possible that life began less than a few hundred million years after the oceans formed, which on a planetary clock is almost immediately.

## What It Leaves Us With

For nearly a billion years, there was a world, and no one in it to call it a world. Then something small, something that did not look like anything, began to copy itself in the dark. Every living thing since, every forest, every whale, every sentence ever written, descends from that.

The Rigveda ended its hymn with a shrug that science has never fully outgrown: perhaps even the one who watches from the highest heaven does not know. What has changed is that we have learned how to ask the question carefully. The long silence before life was not empty. It was where the conditions were being prepared, patiently, for something to hear it.
